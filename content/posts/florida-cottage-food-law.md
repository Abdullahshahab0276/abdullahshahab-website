The Florida cottage food law lets you bake at home and sell to customers without a state food permit, as long as you follow a short list of rules. Most of those rules end up on your label, which is why a home bakery's branding and its legal compliance are really the same job.

**Short answer:** under Florida's cottage food law ([s. 500.80, Florida Statutes](https://m.flsenate.gov/statutes/500.80)), you can make shelf-stable foods like breads, cookies, cakes and candy in your home kitchen and sell up to $250,000 a year with no FDACS license or routine inspection. You must sell directly to consumers (in person, online or by mail), never wholesale, and every product needs a label with your name and address, ingredients, net weight, allergens and the statement "Made in a cottage food operation that is not subject to Florida's food safety regulations."

## What is the cottage food law in Florida?

A cottage food operation is a person, or a business entity, that makes or packages cottage food products at the home of a person who owns it, according to the [FDACS cottage food guide](https://ccmedia.fdacs.gov/content/download/70108/file/Cottage-Food-Operations.pdf). The products must be foods that don't need time or temperature control to stay safe.

The headline rules from the statute and FDACS:

| Rule | What it means for you |
|---|---|
| Sales limit | Up to $250,000 in gross sales a year, counted across all locations and products |
| License | No FDACS license or permit needed |
| Inspections | None routinely; FDACS can investigate after a complaint |
| Where you make it | Your own home kitchen (not a rented kitchen, garage or shed) |
| Where you store it | Inside your home |
| Who you sell to | Consumers directly, never wholesale |
| Local rules | Cities and counties can't ban you, but home-business rules on traffic, parking, noise and signs still apply |

The limit used to be much lower. The [Institute for Justice](https://ij.org/issues/economic-liberty/homemade-food-seller/florida/) notes it was $50,000 before 2021 reforms raised it to $250,000 and let cottage food makers take on business partners.

## Do you need a license to sell baked goods from home in Florida?

Not from the state. FDACS says cottage food operations "do not require a license or permit" from the department and aren't inspected by any state agency. The statute does let FDACS investigate and enter your home if it receives a complaint, so treat your kitchen as if someone might look.

Two things can still apply:

- **Market rules.** A farmers' market or event can set its own requirements. FDACS says you can sell at farmers' markets, flea markets and roadside stands as long as nothing else in your space needs a food permit.
- **Taxes and local registration.** The statute is clear that it doesn't exempt you from tax laws. I cover the general registration steps (EIN, sales tax, local business tax receipt) in [how to start a business in Florida](/blog/how-to-start-a-business-in-florida/). Check with your county tax collector and the [Florida Department of Revenue](https://floridarevenue.com/taxes/registration) for your situation.

Since 2021, the statute says regulation of cottage food is "preempted to the state", so a local ordinance can't prohibit your operation. You do still have to follow the general home-business rules in s. 559.955.

## What foods can you sell under Florida's cottage food law?

The common thread is "non-TCS": no refrigeration needed for safety. Based on FDACS guidance and the Institute for Justice summary, these are allowed:

- Breads, rolls, biscuits, cakes, pastries and cookies
- Candies, hard candy, lollipops, caramel and candy apples, cake pops
- Jams, jellies, preserves and fruit butters made from fresh fruit
- Honey you harvested and packaged yourself
- Dry herbs, seasonings, baking mixes, granola, trail mix, popcorn and nuts
- Nut butters, roasted coffee beans, homemade dry pasta

## What can't you sell under the Florida cottage food law?

FDACS lists several products people often assume are fine:

- **Cream cheese and butter icings** (icing made with margarine, shortening or vegan butter is allowed)
- **Pumpkin and sweet potato pies**, and vegetable butters like pumpkin butter
- **Salsa, barbecue sauce, mustard and other "wet" sauces**
- **Jerky, dried meats and anything with a meat filling**, like empanadas
- **Home-canned produce**, juices and ready-to-drink coffee
- **Foods with hemp, CBD or THC**, unless made in a permitted facility
- **Pet treats.** FDACS says the cottage food rules cover human food only.

If your signature product is on this list, you'll need a permitted kitchen instead.

## Can you sell cottage food online or ship it in Florida?

Yes. Under [s. 500.80(2)](https://m.flsenate.gov/statutes/500.80), you can take orders and payment online or by mail order, and deliver in person, to an event venue, or by USPS or a commercial carrier.

What you can't do: sell wholesale, sell through restaurants, grocery stores or coffee shops, or leave products on consignment. FDACS also says deliveries must be made by you directly, not by a third-party pickup service.

## What has to be on a Florida cottage food label?

This is where law and branding meet. The statute requires every product to be prepackaged with a label showing:

1. The name and address of your cottage food operation (FDACS says a physical address, not a PO box)
2. The product name
3. Ingredients in descending order by weight, including sub-ingredients
4. Net weight or volume
5. Allergen information, following federal rules (milk, eggs, wheat, peanuts, soybeans, fish, shellfish, tree nuts named specifically)
6. Nutrition facts, only if you make a nutrition claim like "low sugar"
7. The statement "Made in a cottage food operation that is not subject to Florida's food safety regulations." in at least 10-point type, in a color that contrasts clearly with the background

For a decorated cake that can't take a sticker, FDACS allows the label information on the box or on the invoice delivered with it.

## How do you brand a home bakery and still follow the label rules?

Most cottage food labels look like an afterthought. You can do better without breaking any rules. Here's what I'd design for a home bakery:

- **A logo that works at sticker size.** Your label might be 2 to 3 inches wide. A bold wordmark or simple script with a small icon reads better than a detailed illustration. My [restaurant logo guide](/blog/restaurant-logo/) covers the same "readable small" test.
- **A front and back system.** Put the logo, product name and one flavor color on the front. Put the required text on a clean back panel with a fixed layout, so every product looks consistent.
- **Space for the 10-point statement.** Design it in from the start.
- **A flavor color code.** One brand color plus an accent per product line helps customers spot their favorite on a market table.
- **Packaging that matches.** Boxes, bags, tissue, thank-you cards and a stamp for kraft boxes. See my [packaging design](/packaging-design/) service.

A bakery logo doesn't need to be cute to sell, but it should match your price point. A pastel script suits celebration cakes; a clean serif with plenty of space suits premium sourdough.

## FAQ

### How much can you make under the Florida cottage food law?
Up to $250,000 in gross sales a year. FDACS can ask for written records to verify your sales.

### Can I sell cupcakes with cream cheese frosting in Florida from home?
No. FDACS lists cream cheese and butter icings as not allowed. Icing made with margarine, shortening or vegan butter is fine.

### Can I sell my baked goods to a coffee shop in Florida?
Not as a cottage food operation. Wholesale, consignment and sales through restaurants or stores aren't allowed. You'd need a permitted food facility.

### Can I form an LLC for my cottage food business?
Yes. FDACS says a cottage food operation can be a person or an entity, as long as the food is made at the home of someone who has an ownership interest in it.
