You can generate a logo in thirty seconds now. Type a prompt, pick the version you like, download a PNG. For a founder watching every dollar, it's tempting.

But there's a question most people only ask after they've printed the business cards: **do you actually own that logo?**

The short answer in 2026: **if the logo was generated entirely by AI, you almost certainly can't copyright it in the United States, and the United Kingdom is heading in the same direction.** If a human designed it, even one who used digital tools along the way, you can.

Here's what that means in plain English, and why it matters more for a logo than for almost anything else your business owns.

## The US position: no human author, no copyright

US copyright law has always protected works of *human* authorship. AI didn't change that rule. It just tested it.

In January 2025, the US Copyright Office published Part 2 of its report on copyright and artificial intelligence, focused specifically on whether AI outputs can be protected. Its conclusion was clear: human authorship is the foundation of copyright, so **works generated entirely by AI are not copyrightable**. The Office also said that using AI as an *assistive* tool doesn't cost a human-made work its protection. What matters is whether a person made the creative decisions.

Crucially for anyone using image generators, the Office's view is that **writing a prompt isn't enough**. Prompts are treated more like instructions than authorship, because the system, not you, decides how those instructions turn into the final image. Ten rounds of re-prompting until you like the result doesn't change that.

The courts agree. In *Thaler v. Perlmutter*, the D.C. Circuit ruled in 2025 that a work created entirely by an AI system couldn't be registered, because the Copyright Act requires a human author. On **March 2, 2026, the US Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal**, which leaves that ruling standing. For now, the human-authorship rule is settled law in the US.

## The UK position: an old loophole that's on its way out

The UK has historically been the outlier. Section 9(3) of the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988 gives a form of protection to "computer-generated works" with no human author, naming as author the person who made the arrangements for the work to be created.

That provision was written decades before generative AI, and it has never been properly tested in court for AI images. In its March 2026 report on copyright and AI, the UK government recommended **removing that protection for works generated by AI with no human author**, while keeping protection for AI-*assisted* works where a person makes the creative choices. No legislation has passed yet, so the old rule technically still exists. But if you're building a brand you intend to keep for ten years, you don't want its centrepiece resting on a clause the government has said it plans to remove.

## Why this matters more for a logo than for anything else

A blog image or a one-off social post being unprotectable is a minor inconvenience. Your logo is different. It's the single asset that appears on everything: your site, your packaging, your invoices, your ads, your signage. It's also the asset competitors and copycats are most likely to imitate.

Without copyright, you have a much weaker footing if:

- **A competitor uses something nearly identical.** Copyright is one of the main tools for stopping someone copying your artwork. If there's no human author, there may be nothing to enforce.
- **You raise money or sell the business.** Investors and buyers check what IP the company actually owns. "We generated it" is not a satisfying answer to "who owns the logo?"
- **Your AI tool produced something close to an existing mark.** Generators learn from enormous amounts of existing imagery. You have no designer's process to show where your mark came from, and no one to vouch that it's original.
- **You want exclusivity.** Nothing stops another founder typing a similar prompt into the same tool and getting a near-twin of your logo.

## "But I can still trademark it, right?"

This is the part most articles skip, so it's worth being precise.

**Copyright and trademark are different things.** Copyright protects a creative work as a piece of art. Trademark protects a sign that tells customers which business a product comes from. A trademark comes from *using* the mark in commerce and, ideally, registering it. It doesn't require a human author.

So yes, an AI-generated logo can potentially function as a trademark. But that's not the whole picture:

- A trademark only protects you in your registered classes and markets. It doesn't stop someone using similar artwork in other contexts the way copyright can.
- Registration takes time and can be refused, especially if the mark is too similar to existing ones, which is a real risk with generator output trained on millions of existing designs.
- The strongest position, and the one serious brands aim for, is **both**: original artwork you own outright, *and* a registered trademark built on it.

A human-designed logo gives you both layers. A purely generated one gives you, at best, one.

## What "human-designed" actually needs to mean

Not every logo with a human nearby counts. Based on the Copyright Office's guidance, the protection follows the creative decisions. So ask any designer, studio or platform these questions before you pay:

1. **Who decides what the mark looks like?** A person should be making the decisions on form, proportion, type and colour, not picking from a list of generated options.
2. **Will you get the source files?** Editable vector files (AI, SVG, EPS, PDF) are a strong sign the mark was actually constructed, not just downloaded as a flat image.
3. **Can they show the process?** Sketches, construction grids, rejected directions, rationale. The [Ovenox logo construction](../../index.html#work) is an example of the kind of evidence a real design process leaves behind.
4. **Is ownership and copyright transfer written down?** You want the scope, deliverables and the transfer of rights confirmed in writing before work starts.
5. **Will they tell you honestly where AI was used, if at all?** In the US, the Copyright Office expects applicants to disclose AI-generated material when registering, so you need to know.
