How Much Does a Logo Cost in 2026? Freelancer vs Agency vs Studio

Logo prices in 2026 run from free AI tools to $10,000+ agency projects. Here's what freelancers, contests, studios and agencies charge, and what you get for it.

ShahabBrand designer · November 10, 2026 · 8 min read

If you’re a founder pricing out a logo, you’ve probably seen quotes that range from the cost of a sandwich to the cost of a used car. Both can be “real” logo prices. They just buy completely different things.

Here’s the short answer for 2026: a logo from an AI tool or template costs $0 to about $200; a freelancer typically charges $100 to $2,500 depending on experience; design contests run from $249 to $1,050; and a branding agency usually starts around $2,500 to $3,000 and can pass $10,000. Independent studios sit between experienced freelancers and agencies.

The number on the quote matters less than what’s inside it: how many concepts, who does the thinking, which files you get, and whether you actually own the result. Let’s go through each option.

What does a logo cost in 2026, at a glance?

These ranges come from current 2026 pricing guides and the platforms’ own pages (sources at the end). They’re market ranges, not fixed prices, and they’re in US dollars.

Option Typical price (USD) What you usually get
AI logo generators and logo makers $0–$200 A generated or template mark, basic exports, no strategy
Fiverr gigs Under $55 to $125+ Varies widely; low tiers are often 1 concept, limited files
Upwork freelancers $15–$30/hr median; top designers $35–$50/hr Depends on the designer; often 2–3 concepts
Design contests (99designs) $249–$1,050 Many concepts from competing designers, one winner
Experienced freelancer (5+ years) $1,500+ Research, original concepts, full vector files
Branding agency $2,500–$10,000+ Strategy, positioning, full identity system, guidelines

For UK founders, one 2026 pricing guide puts a mid-level designer at roughly £250–£1,200 for a logo, and a senior professional at £700–£2,000 or more, broadly in line with the US figures.

How much do AI logo makers and templates cost?

AI generators and template tools cost anywhere from free to around $30 a month. Logo makers and marketplace kits run about $20 to $200. Fiverr’s own logo maker, for example, has plans at $30, $60 and $90.

What you’re paying for is speed. You aren’t paying for anyone to understand your market. The bigger issue is ownership: in the US, a logo generated entirely by AI can’t be copyrighted, and a template mark is usually non-exclusive, so someone else can use something nearly identical. I covered this in detail in Can You Copyright an AI-Generated Logo?.

For a side project or a placeholder while you test an idea, that can be fine. For a brand you plan to build for years, it’s a weak foundation.

How much do freelance logo designers charge?

This is the widest range of all, because “freelancer” covers everyone from a student to a designer with two decades of brand work.

  • Fiverr: Fiverr’s own August 2026 pricing guide lists logo design at under $55 for value gigs, $55–$125 mid-range, and $125+ high-end.
  • Upwork: Upwork puts the median logo designer at $15–$30 an hour, with top designers mostly in the $35–$50 range and some up to $75.
  • By experience: one September 2026 guide breaks freelance logo work down to roughly $300–$500 for designers under two years in, $500–$1,500 for mid-level, and $1,500 and up for senior designers.

The cheap end of the market exists partly because new designers price low to collect reviews. That’s not automatically bad. But at $20–$50, there’s very little time in the budget for research, original drawing or a proper file set. That’s how you end up with a logo that looks fine on a phone screen and falls apart on a sign or an embroidered shirt.

How much does a design contest cost?

On 99designs, logo contests currently come in four packages: Bronze $249, Silver $399, Gold $799 and Platinum $1,050 (sales tax extra). The price covers the platform fee and the winning designer’s prize. If you’d rather hire one designer directly through 99designs, a 5% client platform fee is added to their quote.

The appeal is choice: dozens of concepts from different designers. The trade-off is that most of those designers are working on spec and competing for one prize. That pushes people toward quick, eye-catching concepts over deep thinking about your business. You also need to check carefully that the winning design is original and that the copyright transfers to you.

How much does a branding agency charge for a logo?

Agencies rarely sell “just a logo”. Expect $2,500 to $10,000 or more, and much higher for large agencies working with established companies. DesignRush lists agency logo work from $2,500, with complex professional logos running past $10,000.

That price usually pays for a team: a strategist, a creative director, designers and an account manager. You get research, positioning workshops, a full identity system and brand guidelines. For a funded company with a marketing team, that can be the right call. For a founder at launch, a lot of that budget goes on process and overhead rather than on the work itself.

Where do independent studios fit?

An independent studio, often one senior designer or a very small team, sits in the middle. You typically get agency-style thinking (research, multiple directions, a guidelines document) without paying for the account managers and meetings. Prices vary, but they tend to overlap with experienced freelancers and the lower end of agencies.

The key difference from a cheap freelancer is usually scope. A studio is more likely to quote for a brand identity rather than a single mark: logo variations, colour palette, typography, mockups and a guidelines document. What actually changes the price of a logo?

Whichever option you choose, these are the things that move a quote up or down:

  1. Experience. Senior designers charge more because they make fewer wrong turns and design for how the logo will actually be used.
  2. Number of concepts. Each genuinely different direction is its own creative process. Three concepts cost more than one.
  3. Revision rounds. Two rounds is common. Extra rounds are either billed or built into a higher fee.
  4. Deliverables. A single PNG is cheap. Vector source files, colour variations, a favicon, social profile assets and a guidelines PDF add time.
  5. Strategy and research. Competitor research and positioning are where the agency price comes from.
  6. Timeline. Rush jobs commonly carry a 25–50% premium.
  7. Ownership and usage rights. Make sure the copyright transfer is in writing, whatever you pay.

What are the hidden costs of a cheap logo?

The sticker price is only part of the cost. A logo that’s too cheap often costs more later:

  • Missing files. Without vector files (SVG, AI, EPS or PDF), you’ll pay someone to redraw the logo the first time a printer or sign-maker asks for them.
  • Redesign within a year. If the mark doesn’t work small, in one colour, or on dark backgrounds, you end up redoing it, along with everything it’s printed on.
  • No guidelines. Without rules on colour, type and spacing, every new asset drifts further from the last, and the brand starts to look inconsistent.
  • Ownership questions. Template, AI-only or recycled marks can leave you with nothing to enforce if a competitor copies you.

How much should a startup spend on a logo?

There’s no single right number, but here’s a practical way to think about it:

  • Testing an idea, no revenue yet: a low-cost option is reasonable, as long as you treat it as temporary.
  • Launching a real business you want to grow: budget for a human designer who delivers original concepts, full vector files and basic guidelines. Hundreds, not tens, of dollars.
  • Funded, or entering a crowded premium market: a full brand identity with strategy is worth it, because your brand has to compete on trust from the first day.

Whatever you spend, ask for the deliverables list in writing before you pay.

The bottom line

A logo can cost $0 or $10,000+, and both prices make sense for someone. The useful question isn’t “what’s the cheapest logo?” It’s “what do I need this logo to do, and what does that take?” Compare quotes by what’s included: concepts, revisions, file formats, guidelines and ownership. Don’t compare the headline number alone.
